\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Reading the medians against skill and \texorpdfstring{$1/(2n_{\mathrm{train}})$}{1/(2n)}}

Most KPIs in these leagues carry little leave-one-team-out information about home win beyond the base rate.
Only $\PEFrugbyNinfo$ rugby and $\PEFfootNinfo$ football KPIs are informative under the pre-specified skill rule.
Against that background, the median $D$ versus $(A,B)$ gaps of $\PEFrugbyMedGapDf$ and $\PEFfootMedGapDf$ milli-nats match the prediction $-1/(2n_{\mathrm{train}})$ ($\PEFrugbyHalfN$ and $\PEFfootHalfN$ milli-nats), and the bootstrap intervals for those medians contain the prediction.
That agreement is what aligned, low-signal features should produce: the gap is mostly the cost of a redundant coefficient, not evidence that informative KPIs are aligned.
Where $\Lambda<\PEFLRaic$, the difference is still expected to predict at least as well as the pair out of sample (Supplementary~\cref{sec:si_alignment}).
The median $A$ versus $D$ gap is larger in rugby ($\PEFrugbyMedGapAD$ milli-nats) than in football ($\PEFfootMedGapAD$).
Those statements describe the medians.
They are not an instruction to prefer one column.

\subsection{The cell does not choose the feature}

The Paired Efficiency Factor describes the variance of the difference relative to unpaired measurement.
Paired-design theory motivates testing whether $\eta$ sorts predictive gaps; in these data it does not.
Algebraic $r_A$ is a clearer candidate for the $A$ versus $D$ comparison and shows the expected sign, but it does not replace a direct comparison of $A$, $D$, and $(A,B)$ with an interval.
Rugby and football occupy all four cells and concentrate in Quadrant~3 (\cref{fig:pef_landscape}).
That count describes the cloud.
It does not choose the feature.
A joint model of many KPIs, where $p$ redundant coefficients would cost about $p/(2n_{\mathrm{train}})$, is left for future work.

\subsection{Limitations}

The outcome is home win versus not.
Draws are coded with non-wins.
In these two seasons the home-win rate is $\PEFrugbyHomeWin\%$ in rugby and $\PEFfootHomeWin\%$ in football.
Those rates sit in the usual range for elite rugby and English league football \parencite{morton2006,pollard1986}.
They are a base-rate note for the outcome coding.
They are not a finding of this paper.
Log-loss remains the primary score.
Accuracy is not reported.
Leave-one-team-out holds out one club at a time; the opponent's other matches remain in training, and each match is typically scored twice.
The informative subset is small, so subset correlations are descriptive.
The likelihood-ratio statistic is a full-sample summary under logistic dependence that ignores team recurrence in the reference distribution.
Four exemplars illustrate the cells.
They were selected before the log-loss analysis, after an earlier accuracy-based analysis of the same data.
