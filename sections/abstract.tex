\begin{abstract}
In head-to-head sports such as rugby union and association football, a team's key performance indicator (KPI) can be modelled in absolute terms or relative to its opponent. Relativising a KPI---using the match-level difference between the two teams---sometimes sharpens prediction of the match result and sometimes weakens it, and there has been no general account of when it helps.

We show that the answer depends on two measurable properties of the paired counts: how much the two teams differ in variability, and how strongly their counts move together within a match. We combine these into the Paired Efficiency Factor (PEF), which generalises Fisher's classical efficiency result for paired measurements to the unequal-variance conditions typical of competitive sport. The PEF also connects a KPI's statistical efficiency to how much information its relative form carries about the outcome. Combined KPIs can interact in complex ways in predictive models, so we deliberately analyse each one on its own, establishing the single-indicator case as a foundation for the multivariate models practitioners ultimately use.

Across $\PEFtotalStudies$ KPIs from professional rugby union and association football, the PEF sorts each KPI into one of four regimes that indicate whether relativisation should help. Most sports KPIs fall in the regime where opposing teams' counts move in opposite directions, so absolute measures are usually preferable; yet a relative feature can improve prediction even when it increases variance, showing that statistical efficiency and information content are related but distinct. Representative KPIs from each regime confirm the predicted direction of change in cross-validated accuracy, and the same structure recurs in paired data from healthcare, genomics, finance, and manufacturing.

The PEF turns an ad hoc feature-engineering choice into a transparent, data-informed diagnostic for when to relativise performance metrics in competitive prediction.
\end{abstract}
